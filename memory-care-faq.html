Dementia & memory care FAQ

Questions families ask about memory care.

Caring for a loved one with memory loss can bring many questions. You don't have to figure everything out at once. Here are answers to some of the questions families ask most often.

Considering memory care

How do I know when it's time for memory care?
It may be time to explore additional care when safety, health, daily functioning, or caregiver capacity is becoming difficult to manage at home.

Families may notice:

  • Frequent confusion or wandering
  • Falls or increasing mobility concerns
  • Medication mistakes
  • Difficulty bathing, dressing, or eating
  • Unsafe situations at home
  • Significant changes in behavior
  • Difficulty being safely left alone
  • Increasing caregiver exhaustion

These signs do not automatically mean that memory care is required, but they may indicate that an assessment and discussion with a healthcare professional would be helpful.

You don't have to wait for a crisis to ask questions.

At Olympia Senior Care, we can learn about your loved one's situation, explain the types of support we provide, and discuss whether our setting may be appropriate. Admission depends on an individual assessment of care needs, safety, and whether we can meet those needs within our home and available resources.

What makes Olympia Senior Care different?

Olympia Senior Care is designed to provide a smaller, residential setting with individualized support for older adults, including people living with dementia or other memory-related conditions.

Our approach may include:

  • Person-centered care
  • Low caregiver-to-resident ratios of 1:3 during the day
  • Dementia and memory support
  • Assistance with activities of daily living
  • Meaningful daily activities
  • Montessori-inspired engagement when appropriate
  • Consistent routines
  • Medication assistance or management as permitted by applicable regulations and care plans
  • Supervision and support based on each resident's needs
  • Communication with families
  • Respect for dignity, preferences, and independence

We learn about each resident's history, routines, abilities, preferences, and interests and use that information to guide daily care.

Our home may not be the right setting for every person or every stage of dementia. We discuss care needs, medical conditions, behavioral concerns, and safety requirements with families before admission and as needs change.

Early memory concerns

We're noticing changes in memory. When should we be concerned?
When memory changes begin affecting everyday activities, safety, communication, or judgment, it is reasonable to discuss them with a healthcare provider.

Occasional forgetfulness can occur with aging. More concerning changes may include repeating questions, getting lost in familiar places, struggling with familiar tasks, difficulty managing medications or finances, or noticeable changes in judgment or behavior.

Memory changes can have many possible causes, including medication effects, medical conditions, depression, sleep problems, or dementia. A healthcare professional can help evaluate the situation.

What are the early signs of Alzheimer's or dementia?

Possible signs include:

  • Repeating questions or stories
  • Difficulty finding words
  • Confusion about time or place
  • Trouble completing familiar activities
  • Misplacing items in unusual places
  • Withdrawal from familiar activities
  • Changes in mood or personality
  • Increasing difficulty with everyday self-care
  • Problems with planning, judgment, or problem-solving

One symptom alone does not establish a diagnosis of dementia.

How do I talk to my loved one about memory problems?
Use a calm, respectful approach and focus on support rather than proving that something is wrong.

You might say:

“I've noticed that some things seem a little harder lately. I'm concerned about you, and I'd like us to talk with your doctor together.”

Choose a quiet time, allow your loved one to respond, and avoid arguing about specific mistakes. If the conversation becomes distressing, consider pausing and trying again later.

Who should we talk to first?
A primary care provider is often a good place to start.

The provider can review symptoms, medications, medical history, and possible contributing conditions and determine whether additional evaluation or referral is appropriate.

What happens during a dementia evaluation?

An evaluation may include:

  • Medical and medication history
  • Discussion of symptoms and changes in daily functioning
  • Cognitive or memory testing
  • Physical and neurological examination
  • Laboratory testing
  • Brain imaging when clinically appropriate

The evaluation varies by person. Olympia Senior Care does not diagnose dementia or replace a healthcare provider's evaluation.

Can dementia be treated or slowed?
Some symptoms and causes of cognitive impairment may be treatable, and certain treatments may help some people with specific types or stages of dementia.

There is currently no cure for most forms of dementia. A healthcare provider can discuss diagnosis-specific treatment options, potential benefits, risks, and limitations.

Supportive care, including appropriate routines, social connection, physical activity when safe, nutrition, meaningful activities, and environmental support, may help promote comfort and quality of life. These measures do not stop or reverse dementia.

Staying connected

Can I visit my loved one?
Yes. Family involvement is generally encouraged, subject to the resident's preferences, health needs, and reasonable house guidelines.

Your loved one's relationships remain important after moving into care. We can discuss visiting routines and any considerations related to privacy, illness, safety, or the resident's comfort.

Can I bring familiar things from home?
Usually, familiar belongings can help personalize a resident's space and support comfort.

Consider photographs, favorite books, music, blankets, artwork, or other meaningful items. Please discuss larger, valuable, fragile, electrical, or potentially unsafe items with the care team before bringing them.

What if my loved one doesn't recognize me?
Recognition may change over time, but a visit can still be meaningful.

Focus less on testing memory and more on creating a calm, positive interaction. A familiar voice, favorite song, smile, or quiet time together may be comforting, although responses vary from person to person.

What if my loved one doesn't want to see us?
Resistance may reflect confusion, anxiety, fatigue, pain, overstimulation, or difficulty processing the visit.

Avoid forcing an interaction. The care team can help identify a better time, shorter visit, quieter setting, or different approach. If refusal is persistent or accompanied by other changes, we may recommend discussing it with the resident's healthcare provider.

Can family participate in meals and activities?
Family participation may be possible depending on the resident's preferences, the activity, and the home's routines and safety requirements.

Families may be able to share meals, listen to music, participate in activities, take walks, or spend time together. Please coordinate with the care team in advance when needed.

How will our family know if something changes?
We communicate significant changes in condition, care needs, incidents, or concerns according to the resident's care plan, applicable requirements, and the family's designated communication preferences.

Families should tell us about changes they observe as well. Communication does not replace medical evaluation, and urgent symptoms may require emergency services or contact with the resident's healthcare provider.

Family communication is an important part of coordinated care.

Adjusting to a new home

How long does it take to adjust?
There is no standard adjustment period.

Some residents settle into a new routine within days, while others need weeks or longer. Changes in confusion, mood, sleep, appetite, or behavior can occur during a transition and should be monitored.

Consistent routines, familiar items, calm communication, and gradual support may help. If changes are severe, persistent, or medically concerning, the resident's healthcare provider should be contacted.

Does moving my loved one mean I'm giving up on them?
Choosing additional care does not mean you have stopped caring.

As needs change, care at home may become difficult or unsafe for one person or family to manage. A residential setting may provide assistance with daily activities, supervision, routines, and companionship within the services it is equipped and authorized to provide.

The decision should be based on the resident's needs, preferences, safety, available support, and the capabilities of the selected setting.

Will my loved one feel abandoned?
A move can be emotionally difficult, and residents may respond in different ways.

Family visits, familiar belongings, consistent communication, and a gradual introduction to routines may help some residents. We cannot guarantee that a transition will be easy or that distress will not occur. We monitor adjustment and discuss concerns with families and healthcare professionals when appropriate.

What should I say when my loved one asks to go home?
Try acknowledging the feeling before offering reassurance or redirection.

You might say:

“I know you miss home. You're safe, and I'm here with you.”

You can also ask what they miss and offer a familiar activity, snack, music, or conversation. If the request is frequent or accompanied by distress, discuss it with the care team.

How do I deal with the guilt?
Guilt is common, but needing help does not mean you have failed.

Dementia care can become complex and demanding. Seeking additional support may be one way to respond to changing needs. Families may also benefit from support groups, counseling, respite resources, or guidance from healthcare and social-service professionals.

The Olympia Senior Care approach

How do you personalize care?
We gather information about each resident and use it to guide care within the services we provide.

We may consider:

  • Personal history
  • Interests and hobbies
  • Daily routines
  • Communication preferences
  • Mobility
  • Personal care needs
  • Nutrition and eating preferences
  • Medications and related support needs
  • Sleep patterns
  • Behavioral changes
  • Meaningful activities
  • Family relationships
  • Cultural, spiritual, and personal preferences

Care plans may change as a resident's condition, preferences, or needs change. Families and healthcare professionals may be involved as appropriate.

How do you respond to confusion or agitation in residents with Alzheimer's and other forms of memory loss?
We try to understand what may be contributing to the behavior before deciding how to respond.

Possible contributors include pain, illness, fear, fatigue, hunger, loneliness, overstimulation, frustration, medication effects, or confusion.

Depending on the situation, our response may include reassurance, validation, redirection, reducing stimulation, offering a familiar activity, maintaining routines, or addressing an immediate comfort need. We document and communicate significant or recurring changes as appropriate.

Some behaviors require medical assessment or a higher level of care. We do not promise that every behavior can be prevented or managed in our setting.

What is your approach to medications?
Medication support is provided according to the resident's care plan, applicable regulations, and the scope of services available at Olympia Senior Care.

Depending on the resident and applicable requirements, support may include reminders, assistance, observation, documentation, or medication administration by appropriately authorized personnel. All of our care partners are extensively trained to administer medications as ordered by the physician.

Medication decisions, prescribing, dosage changes, and clinical monitoring remain the responsibility of the resident's healthcare providers and pharmacy. We coordinate with family, provider, and pharmacy to ensure that we provide current medication information and report changes promptly.

How do you keep residents engaged?
We offer activities based on residents' interests, abilities, preferences, and daily condition. Participation is encouraged but not forced.

Activities may include:

  • Music
  • Art and crafts
  • Gardening
  • Walking or other appropriate movement
  • Cooking or food-related activities
  • Games and puzzles
  • Reading
  • Storytelling
  • Gentle movement
  • Everyday household activities
  • Seasonal traditions

We may use person-centered and Montessori-inspired techniques when appropriate, such as offering familiar tasks, choices, and opportunities for purposeful participation. Activities are adapted for safety and ability. They are intended to support engagement and quality of life, not to diagnose, cure, or reliably slow dementia.

More about life enrichment

What happens as dementia progresses?
Care needs may increase over time, and the care plan may need to be revised.

Residents may require more assistance with mobility, personal care, communication, nutrition, medications, supervision, or daily activities. We review changing needs with the resident, family, and healthcare professionals as appropriate.

Olympia Senior Care may not be able to provide every level of medical, behavioral, or physical care. If a resident's needs exceed what our home can safely and appropriately provide, we will discuss available options with the resident's representative and care team.

Our goal is to provide respectful, individualized support within the limits of our setting, staffing, training, and applicable requirements.

Do you use technology in dementia care?
Technology may be used when it is appropriate, accessible, and helpful for the individual resident.

Examples may include music, reminiscence, entertainment, family communication, cognitive engagement, or telehealth access when available and clinically appropriate.

Technology is not a substitute for in-person care, supervision, clinical assessment, or emergency services. Use depends on the resident's preferences, abilities, privacy, consent, and safety considerations.
